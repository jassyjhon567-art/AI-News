Disclosure: I built GigLedger , a true-profit tracker for gig drivers. I am not a gig driver, and I do not pretend to be one. I built this after hearing the same frustration from drivers again and again: the app says you made good money, but the car, the gas and the tax bill say otherwise. Thanksgiving week is the perfect example. It looks like a great week to drive — everyone is ordering, tips are supposed to be generous, and the apps are busy. Some of it is true. But the week also has traps that only show up when you count miles. The holiday trap: busy does not mean profitable On Thanksgiving Day itself, many restaurants close early or close completely. The ones still open are spread further apart, so the miles between orders grow. A $14 order that would be 4 miles on a normal Tuesday can become a $14 order over 11 miles on a holiday, with a long wait in between. The payout screen does not show that change. Your odometer does. Grocery delivery tells the same story from the other side. Instacart and Walmart Spark are slammed in the days before Thanksgiving, and the batches look big. But a big batch means a long time in a crowded store, then a long drive to a customer who may live far from the store. If you judge the batch by its total alone, you will take batches that pay less per hour than a quieter day. Black Friday is the same test in retail form: long drives to malls and warehouses, heavy traffic, and orders that look good until you add up the deadhead miles back to where the next order is. The only math that matters: true profit True profit is simple, and that is the point: Gross pay minus mileage cost minus other expenses equals true profit. True profit divided by hours equals true hourly pay. True profit divided by miles equals true pay per mile. SAMPLE math (not a real shift): DoorDash $96.40 gross, 4.5 hours, 71 miles at $0.70 per mile. The mileage cost is $49.70, so true profit is $46.70, or $10.38 per true hour. The app total looked like $21.42 an hour. The miles cut it in half. Use that same test on a Thanksgiving shift. If the gross is higher than usual but the miles are much higher too, the true hourly rate may be lower than a normal week. That is not a reason to stay home — it is a reason to be picky. A holiday floor: set it before you leave Before a holiday shift, pick two numbers and write them down: A minimum pay per mile. If an order pays less than your floor after miles, decline it, however big the total looks. A minimum true pay per hour. When the orders fall below it for a while, stop. A short morning of good orders beats a long day of chasing bad ones. Then log every shift the same way: gross, hours, miles, gas and other expenses. At the end of the day, you will know whether the holiday paid you — or whether it paid your car. Expenses and tax: the part drivers forget on holidays Holiday weeks also pile up the small costs: tolls on a longer route, a phone charger, a hot bag, parking while you wait. Photograph receipts the same day, while you still remember what they were for. At year-end, that habit turns into a clean expense record instead of a guessing game. And remember that no tax is taken out of gig payouts. Set tax money aside from true profit, not from gross, and look at quarterly estimated payments if your gig income is more than a small side amount. A complete mileage log — one you can export as a PDF — is what makes the mileage deduction real at tax time. The IRS standard mileage rate covers gas, wear, tires, oil and depreciation in one number, so do not also deduct gas separately when you use it. Outside the US: use your own country's rate Thanksgiving is a US holiday, but the true-profit test travels. If you drive outside the US, use your own country's official mileage rate and your local platforms — Deliveroo and Just Eat in the UK, Menulog in Australia, SkipTheDishes in Canada, Mr D in South Africa — and do not apply US tax rules to a non-US shift. In the UK, for example, the mileage log matters at HMRC's 45p per mile. Australia has the ATO cents-per-kilometre rate, Canada has CRA rules, New Zealand has IRD rules, and South Africa has SARS rules. The principle is the same everywhere: count every business mile, including the drive to the pickup and the miles between orders, because those are the miles that decide whether a shift paid.
