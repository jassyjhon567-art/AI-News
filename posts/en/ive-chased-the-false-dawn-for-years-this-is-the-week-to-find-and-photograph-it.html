Chasing the 'False Dawn': Why This Week Offers the Perfect Window to Spot Zodiacal Light

www.space.com · Fri, 11 Sep 2026 21:00:00 +0000

September’s new moon provides optimal dark skies for skywatchers and photographers to observe the elusive zodiacal light, also known as the 'false dawn,' rising above the eastern horizon.

For stargazers and astrophotographers, the arrival of September’s new moon brings a rare and magical skywatching opportunity. As the moon vanishes from the night sky, a pristine, dark window opens to observe the elusive phenomenon known as zodiacal light, often referred to as the "false dawn." This ghostly celestial glow is caused by sunlight reflecting off microscopic interplanetary dust particles left behind by comets and asteroids along the plane of the solar system.

Appearing as a faint, towering cone or pyramid of hazy light, the phenomenon stretches upward along the ecliptic from the eastern horizon well before actual morning twilight breaks. To spot it, observers must seek out locations far removed from municipal light pollution, as even modest ambient glow can wash out the delicate feature. The optimal viewing time occurs roughly eighty to one hundred and twenty minutes before sunrise, when the sky remains pitch-black and the dust column tilts high into the atmosphere.

For those aiming to photograph the spectacle, a camera with manual controls mounted on a sturdy tripod is essential. Using a wide-angle lens, an open aperture, and a high ISO setting with an exposure of several seconds will easily capture the ethereal pyramid against background stars. Because the ecliptic forms a steep angle with the eastern horizon during the autumn months in the Northern Hemisphere, this week offers one of the best opportunities of the entire year to witness this subtle cosmic display.
