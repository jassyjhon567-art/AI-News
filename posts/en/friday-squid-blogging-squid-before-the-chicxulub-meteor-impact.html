Prehistoric Squid Flourished Worldwide Prior to Chicxulub Asteroid Impact, Study Shows

www.schneier.com · 2026-10-16T21:06:47Z

New research reveals that prehistoric squid were distributed across oceans worldwide prior to the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction event triggered by the Chicxulub asteroid impact.

Recent paleontological research reveals that ancient squid inhabited oceans across the globe long before the catastrophic Chicxulub meteor strike. The massive impact, which defines the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary approximately 66 million years ago, triggered a catastrophic mass extinction event that wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs alongside an estimated three-quarters of Earth's plant and animal species. Nevertheless, emerging findings indicate that cephalopod lineages, including the ancestors of modern squid, had already achieved a widespread global distribution well before this ecological crisis.

The global presence of these ancient creatures highlights a remarkable chapter in evolutionary resilience. While other prominent marine groups of the era, most notably the heavily shelled ammonites, were completely eradicated by the environmental aftermath of the meteor strike, early squid ancestors managed to persist. Scientists examining the historical distribution and fossil evidence suggest that these versatile cephalopods inhabited a wide range of oceanic depths and thermal zones, enabling them to establish self-sustaining populations in distinct corners of the prehistoric world.

These findings offer significant insights into how marine biodiversity endured and rebounded across one of the most severe extinction boundaries in geological history. By demonstrating that squid were already globally ubiquitous prior to the asteroid's arrival, researchers can more accurately reconstruct prehistoric marine food webs and trace the biological adaptations that facilitated cephalopod survival. Understanding how soft-bodied marine organisms weathered such extreme global disturbances continues to provide essential context for the evolutionary origins of modern ocean ecosystems.
