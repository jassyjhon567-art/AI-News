Polygenic Embryo Screening: How Much Would You Pay for a Smarter Baby?

www.economist.com · Mon, 10 Aug 2026 19:17:41 +0000

Biotechnology startups claim embryo screening can predict complex traits like IQ, but experts warn of ethical risks and scientific unreliability.

A growing number of modern biotechnology startups are now offering prospective parents the unprecedented ability to screen embryos for a wide spectrum of traits, extending beyond rare monogenic conditions to complex characteristics such as high intelligence and susceptibility to common diseases. By utilizing advanced polygenic risk scoring methods, these commercial entities analyze millions of genetic markers obtained from in vitro fertilization (IVF) embryos. Proponents of the technology maintain that such comprehensive genetic insights empower families to make well-informed reproductive decisions, potentially decreasing the incidence of hereditary health conditions and optimizing the overall well-being of future children.

Despite these bold claims, leading geneticists and bioethicists remain skeptical regarding the scientific reliability and ethical implications of polygenic embryo screening. Critics highlight that complex human traits like cognitive ability are influenced by thousands of interacting genes alongside substantial environmental factors, making accurate prediction incredibly difficult. Furthermore, selecting embryos based on complex probabilistic scores carries severe risks, including the unintended selection of negative traits, unforeseen biological trade-offs, and the unnecessary rejection of otherwise healthy viable embryos during the IVF process.

Beyond the clinical uncertainties, the commercialization of trait selection introduces profound social concerns regarding equity and human enhancement. Ethics experts warn that expensive genetic screening services could widen existing socioeconomic divides, effectively creating a tiered society where wealthy individuals can pay to engineer biological advantages for their offspring. As regulatory bodies struggle to keep pace with these swift technological developments, global policymakers and society at large face an urgent debate over where to draw the boundaries between legitimate disease prevention and controversial human trait selection.
