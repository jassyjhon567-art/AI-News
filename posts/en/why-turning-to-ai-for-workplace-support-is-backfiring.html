Why turning to AI for workplace support is backfiring

We’ve all been on a quest to make our workdays a little easier. For years, we’ve been adopting new tools, automating tedious tasks, and stripping away the barriers that slow us down. With AI , we’re closer to that frictionless day than ever before. Yet, in our rush to make work as smooth as possible (which, don’t get me wrong, I highly value), might we risk dissolving the social fabric that helps teams thrive? Workday’s recent research, the Human Connection Workplace Index , shows that 14% of employees overall, and 20% of Gen Z, have taken time off in the past year due to feelings of loneliness or isolation. To understand why efficiency doesn’t necessarily lead to well-being, I wanted to look closely at the work of Constance Hadley, PhD, an organizational psychologist and the founder of the Institute for Life at Work. She has spent years studying the drivers of employee isolation. Her empirical work on workplace loneliness demonstrates that true human connection is more complex than simply co-existing in a physical or digital workspace. When I sat down to chat with Hadley about how emerging technologies intersect with workplace isolation, she captured our new reality perfectly: “We now have AI as a potential substitute for human connection, and it’s a new player in the relationship dynamic at work.” THE AI SUPPORT PARADOX In her most recent research , published in Harvard Business Review , Hadley found a trend. Three-quarters of regular AI users reported turning to AI for career coaching and friendship, support traditionally provided by colleagues. Strikingly, employees frequently described these tools as empathetic, patient, and nonjudgmental. However, half of these workers also reported feeling moderately or highly lonely on the job. Workday’s research reinforces these findings, revealing that one-third of employees rarely or never have personal conversations with their colleagues, and one in five Gen Z employees say they feel lonelier since AI tools were introduced in the workplace. Both studies seem to point to the same realization: As technology becomes the path of least social resistance, the human interaction that builds cohesion might be fading away. 4 RELATIONAL RISKS In her research, Hadley outlines four specific relational disruptions that occur when software replaces human collaboration. The first is when companies may downsize their workforce during restructuring. In this case, independent work may increase, leading to greater workplace isolation. The second is social stamina atrophy, when constantly interacting with a nonjudgmental and patient chatbot may make human conversations seem more intimidating or exhausting. In fact, our Workday research shows that, since the widespread adoption of AI, almost one-third of employees say they’re losing key social muscles as a result. These reports include at least one warning sign: less patience for small talk, trouble reading colleagues’ cues, heightened anxiety around unscripted check-ins, or a declining ability to resolve conflict face-to-face. The third disruption happens when workers default to AI for answers rather than asking a peer, and therefore, opportunities to build trust and reciprocity disappear. Finally, the fourth disruption is what Hadley describes as an “existential loneliness” that’s triggered when you realize you are confiding in an artificial program, incapable of true presence. Her description of the illusion of intimacy reminds me of a point I once heard psychotherapist Esther Perel say, about how many people have hundreds of online “friends,” but no one to feed their cat when they go out of town. When your go-to confidant at work is an algorithm, that sense of solitude can be profound. RETHINK OUR NEEDS It’s also important not to miscalculate who is most at risk. During our conversation, Hadley shared a crucial insight regarding personality and social needs. She described loneliness as “the gap between what you’re seeking and what you’re getting back.” So, although we may often assume that introverts, for example, are thriving in this era of remote work and chatbots, she said, “I’ve consistently seen across my studies that introverts are even lonelier than extroverts. Even if they do prefer working alone, that doesn’t mean they don’t need other humans, and we can’t assume they’re doing fine on their own.” When Hadley surveyed workers across all demographics to see what they wanted from their employers to feel connected, the consensus was clear. “Employees want the five to 10 minutes of meeting chit-chat, communal lunches, and the occasional team outing.” She also, however, found that the loneliest employees are also the ones usually most reluctant to engage in these team rituals. She pointed out that it’s easy to fall into a negative pattern when you’re lonely. “This is an important message for leaders to understand: It’s not an automatic “if you build it, they will come” situation. You actually need to do targeted outreach to overcome a lot of the hesitation.” What happens when employees feel like their leaders care, and are committed to their well-being? Hadley says employees then reported higher job satisfaction, more positive attitudes towards AI, higher productivity from AI, and a stronger intention to stay at their company. HUMAN-CENTERED LEADERSHIP The solution is not to reject AI, but to use its efficiency to create a richer social architecture. Leaders can protect their culture by keeping vital functions—like onboarding, mentoring, conflict resolution, and performance feedback—strictly human-led. For Hadley, reinvesting the time saved by these AI tools back into your team is vital, especially when social isolation is believed to be the cause of so many health concerns today. The World Health Organization estimates that loneliness is associated with 100 deaths every hour. “Our lives are so digitized now that the workplace is often our only opportunity for social connection. Make it your business to invest that saved time back into your work culture. For leaders, it is a real opportunity that you are uniquely positioned to take up, and it could make a huge difference.” Carrie Varoquiers is chief impact officer at Workday.
