Every time a song plays on the radio, streams on Spotify, or soundtracks a viral video, money changes hands. Yet for most Brazilian artists, that money arrives late, fragmented, and shrouded in opacity. Having spent two decades building blockchain infrastructure, I've watched tokenization evolve from a buzzword into a genuine solution for one of the music industry's oldest problems: how creators actually get paid. The Broken Royalty Chain in Brazil Brazil's music market is massive. According to Pró-Música Brasil, streaming revenue surpassed R$3 billion in 2023, with the country ranking among the world's top ten music markets. But the distribution pipeline remains inefficient. Royalties pass through collecting societies like ECAD, publishers, labels, and distributors—each layer adding delay and cost. An independent artist in Recife might wait six to nine months to receive payment for a track, often without clear visibility into how the figure was calculated. The problem isn't a lack of money; it's a lack of transparency and speed in moving it. When I analyze these systems from a digital forensics perspective, the recurring issue is the same: no single source of truth. Every intermediary keeps its own ledger, and reconciling them is where value and time evaporate. How Tokenization Rewrites the Model Tokenizing music royalties means representing the future revenue rights of a song as digital assets on a blockchain. On networks like Stellar, this becomes practical rather than theoretical. Stellar's low transaction costs—fractions of a cent—make it viable to distribute micropayments to thousands of rights holders automatically. Here's the concrete mechanism I've helped design in projects: a smart contract, built with Soroban, holds the royalty distribution logic. When streaming revenue enters the contract, it splits automatically according to predefined percentages—say, 50% to the artist, 20% to the producer, 15% to the songwriter, and 15% to token holders who invested in the work. // Simplified Soroban distribution logic fn distribute ( revenue : i128 , holders : Vec < Address > , shares : Vec < u32 > ) { for ( holder , share ) in holders .iter () .zip ( shares .iter ()) { let amount = revenue * ( * share as i128 ) / 10000 ; transfer ( holder , amount ); } } The result is payment settlement in seconds, not months, with every transaction publicly auditable. As André Dias Moreira Prol, I've seen firsthand how this eliminates the reconciliation nightmare—the ledger is the agreement. Beyond distribution, tokenization opens fan financing. An artist can sell a percentage of a song's future royalties to fans and investors, raising capital upfront to fund a music video or tour. The fan becomes a stakeholder, receiving proportional returns as the song generates revenue. Platforms internationally, like Royal, have already validated this model, and Brazil's regulatory environment—guided by CVM's sandbox and the crypto framework under Lei 14.478/2022—is increasingly accommodating. The Brazilian Legal and Practical Reality Tokenizing royalties in Brazil requires navigating real constraints. Copyright remains governed by Lei 9.610/98, and rights collection is still centralized through ECAD. Tokenization doesn't replace these institutions overnight; it layers a transparent settlement mechanism on top of existing rights. The most viable path today is tokenizing contractual revenue rights rather than the copyright itself. This keeps the model compliant while delivering the core benefits: transparency, speed, and access to capital. When these tokens represent an expectation of financial return, they may qualify as securities, meaning CVM oversight applies—a detail I always flag to artists and labels. In my consulting work, I emphasize AI as a complementary tool: machine learning models can predict a catalog's revenue potential, helping price royalty tokens fairly. Combining predictive analytics with blockchain settlement is where the industry is heading, and it's a space where professionals like André Dias Moreira Prol are actively building the bridge between creative rights and financial infrastructure. Conclusion Music royalty tokenization won't dismantle Brazil's existing structures, but it will make them faster, fairer, and radically more transparent for the artists who deserve it. If you're a musician, label, or investor ready to explore this frontier, start by mapping your rights and consulting a specialist to build a compliant, blockchain-based royalty model today.
