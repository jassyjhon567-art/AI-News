US Lawmakers Urge Regulators to Crack Down on Wildfire Prediction Markets

www.fastcompany.com · 2026-08-06T20:09:57

US lawmakers are urging federal regulators to curb prediction markets that allow users to bet on wildfires and natural disasters due to severe ethical and safety risks.

A bipartisan group of U.S. lawmakers, led by Oregon Senator Jeff Merkley, is urging federal regulators to crack down on the growing trend of prediction markets allowing users to bet on natural disasters and public emergencies. In a formal letter to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), the senators demanded common-sense safeguards to stop speculative platforms from commodifying human suffering. The plea comes amid devastating wildfires in the Pacific Northwest, where hazardous air quality and displacement have heightened public policy and ethical concerns regarding companies profiting from ongoing catastrophes.

The lawmakers warned that these unregulated betting platforms create severe perverse incentives, including the potential for arson, public safety threats, and insider trading. During destructive wildfires in Los Angeles, more than a million dollars circulated on prediction sites like Polymarket as gamblers placed highly specific bets on fire duration and destruction levels. Officials expressed grave concern that high financial stakes could tempt individuals to actively instigate or exacerbate real-world disasters, or leverage confidential information, simply to secure lucrative payouts from these speculative wagers.

As federal pressure mounts, individual states are taking independent legal steps to restrict prediction platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket. Lawmakers in California and Utah have introduced targeted legislation to outlaw betting on war, terrorism, assassination, and illegal activities, viewing these markets as direct threats to national security and community safety. State authorities maintain that speculative disaster betting remains a form of illegal gambling that must be strictly regulated before dangerous financial incentives result in irreversible real-world harm.
