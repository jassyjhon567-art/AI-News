Discovery of Two Rapidly Spinning Asteroids Challenges Understanding of Space Rocks

www.newscientist.com · Fri, 09 Oct 2026 17:00:00 +0000

The discovery of two large asteroids rotating at unprecedented speeds in the asteroid belt suggests these space rocks possess greater internal strength than existing structural models predict.

Astronomers have identified two massive asteroids in the solar system's main asteroid belt that are rotating at extraordinarily high velocities, spinning at rates comparable to a fast fairground thrill ride. While smaller fragments in space can occasionally rotate rapidly without breaking apart, celestial bodies of this scale generally take several hours, or even multiple days, to complete a single full rotation. Consequently, encountering large objects spinning at such breakneck speeds represents a rare and deeply puzzling observation that defies standard celestial expectations and warrants rigorous scrutiny.

This revelation fundamentally challenges prevailing scientific theories regarding the internal structure and physical cohesion of large asteroids. Conventional planetary science holds that most mid-to-large-sized asteroids are essentially "rubble piles"—loose aggregations of boulders, dust, and rock fragments bound together only by weak mutual gravity. Under these standard assumptions, any rubble pile rotating at such an intense pace should experience overwhelming centrifugal forces that would inevitably tear the object apart, flinging its constituent material outward into the void of interplanetary space.

Because these two asteroids remain structurally intact, researchers believe our current models of asteroid mechanics may be fundamentally flawed or incomplete. The objects must possess far greater internal tensile strength than anticipated, suggesting they may either be solid monolithic slabs of rock or held together by powerful cohesive forces within their material matrix. As astronomers carry out further observations, resolving this mystery could significantly alter scientific understanding of how small planetary bodies form, evolve, and survive collisions across the solar system over billions of years.
