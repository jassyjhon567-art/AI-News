Japan Set to Launch Landmark Mission to Mars's Largest Moon, Phobos

www.newscientist.com · Fri, 16 Oct 2026 17:00:00 +0000

Japan's upcoming Martian Moons eXploration mission to Phobos aims to return surface samples that could finally resolve a century-old mystery regarding the origin of Mars's moons.

Japan is preparing for an ambitious interplanetary voyage with the upcoming launch of its Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft, officially scheduled for liftoff on 20 October. Targeting Phobos, the larger of Mars's two mysterious natural satellites, the mission represents a critical milestone in robotic space exploration. The spacecraft is engineered to journey to the Martian system, safely touch down on the rugged surface of Phobos, collect crucial soil samples, and navigate back to Earth to deliver them for rigorous scientific scrutiny.

For over a century, planetary scientists and astronomers have grappled with the elusive origins of Phobos and its smaller companion, Deimos. Two primary scientific hypotheses dominate the discourse: either the moons are ancient interplanetary asteroids captured by Mars's gravitational field, or they are the coalesced fragments of debris hurled into orbit after a massive cosmic impact with Mars. By retrieving pristine regolith directly from the moon's surface, the MMX mission aims to reveal its exact chemical and isotopic makeup, effectively settling the decades-old astronomical debate.

Beyond solving the origin story of Phobos, the mission promises broader implications for understanding planetary formation and the evolutionary history of our solar system. Analyzing the returned samples could also offer secondary insights into Mars's ancient atmospheric conditions and the transport of volatile elements like water. Successfully retrieving these extraterrestrial specimens will equip international research laboratories with pristine material, marking a major triumph for global space exploration and laying a solid foundation for future crewed journeys to the Red Planet.
