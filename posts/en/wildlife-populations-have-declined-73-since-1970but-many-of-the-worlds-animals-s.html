Global Wildlife Populations Drop 73% Since 1970, Though Many Species Remain Uncounted

phys.org · Sun, 11 Oct 2026 20:30:01 EDT

WWF's Living Planet Index reports an average 73 percent decline in monitored global wildlife populations since 1970, though experts emphasize that many animal species still remain uncounted.

Global wildlife populations have suffered an alarming average decline of 73 percent over the past five decades, according to the latest Living Planet Index published by the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF). Updated every two years, this comprehensive assessment serves as one of the world's most vital barometers for evaluating the changing state of the natural environment. The latest findings emphasize an escalating ecological crisis driven by continuous habitat loss, climate change, and broader human pressures spanning terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems worldwide.

Crucially, scientists and conservation experts emphasize that this headline statistic does not indicate that nearly three-quarters of all individual wild animals on the planet have been wiped out. Instead, the measurement reflects an average proportional reduction across thousands of monitored wildlife populations between 1970 and the present day. While some monitored species have remained resilient or recovered due to targeted protection efforts, a vast majority of tracked groups have experienced steep contractions, dragging down the overall global index and signaling broader ecosystem instability.

At the same time, the report highlights a critical limitation in modern ecological science: a significant portion of the Earth's animal life is still not being routinely counted. Immense data gaps persist across remote geographical regions and among under-surveyed species, notably invertebrates and smaller organisms that form the foundation of ecological webs. Specialists caution that while the documented 73 percent downturn reveals profound systemic strain, unmonitored populations may be facing comparable or even more severe risks in an increasingly fragile natural world, requiring immediate international action and expanded scientific tracking.
