How a Dry Martian Meteorite Unlocks Secrets of Ancient Water on Mars

phys.org · Thu, 13 Aug 2026 15:00:01 EDT

Researchers using neutron and X-ray tomography have discovered hydrogen-rich regions inside a Martian meteorite, providing key evidence of ancient water-rock interactions on Mars.

Scientists have uncovered compelling evidence of ancient water on Mars by closely analyzing an apparently dry Martian meteorite. In a pioneering international study conducted with key participation from the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI), researchers succeeded in locating macroscopic hydrogen-rich regions concealed deep within the rock's interior, shedding new light on the Red Planet's geological past.

Detecting historical signatures of moisture where no liquid water currently remains required advanced non-destructive imaging methods. The research team employed a sophisticated combination of neutron and X-ray tomography to peer inside the sample without compromising its structural integrity. Neutrons are particularly sensitive to hydrogen atoms, allowing the team to map previously invisible hydrous mineral phases trapped within the mineral matrix.

These newly discovered hydrogen concentrations provide strong evidence for extensive water-rock interactions during the early stages of Mars's planetary evolution. By demonstrating that liquid water once actively reacted with Martian crustal rocks, the findings offer vital clues about the planet's early climate and volatile history, while also refining scientists' understanding of ancient environmental conditions that might have once supported microbial life.
