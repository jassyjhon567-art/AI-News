How Animal Feces Helped Fuel Complex Life on Earth Millions of Years Ago

www.popsci.com · Wed, 05 Aug 2026 14:02:00 -0400

Around 540 million years ago, nutrient-rich animal feces transported essential elements to the ocean floor, playing a vital role in sustaining the evolution of complex life.

Around 540 million years ago, during the Cambrian explosion, Earth's oceans experienced a dramatic rise in complex animal life. Scientists point to a surprisingly humble biological product as a key driver of this evolutionary boom: animal feces. As early multicellular organisms evolved and began to ingest larger quantities of food, they began producing nutrient-dense fecal matter that sank rapidly through the water column, fundamentally altering marine ecosystems and global nutrient cycles.

Prior to this evolutionary milestone, microscopic organisms dominated the oceans, and organic material was largely decomposed near the surface before reaching deeper realms. The emergence of larger animals capable of producing compact fecal pellets transformed this system entirely. These dense waste pellets acted as efficient delivery vehicles, transporting crucial nutrients like carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus directly down to the seabed. This influx enriched the benthic zone, creating fertile environments where diverse bottom-dwelling organisms could thrive, feed, and rapidly diversify.

Ultimately, this strategic redistribution of organic material helped stabilize ancient chemical cycles and supported the expansion of intricate food webs across the planet's oceans. By continuously recycling essential elements and spreading nourishment to previously barren environments, prehistoric animal waste played a foundational role in shaping the trajectory of life on Earth. Without this seemingly simple ecological mechanism, marine ecosystems might never have sustained the evolutionary momentum that ultimately paved the way for modern biological diversity.
