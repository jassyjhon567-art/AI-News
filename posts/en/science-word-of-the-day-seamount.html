Science Word of the Day: What Is a Seamount and Why Is It Important?

www.livescience.com · Tue, 28 Jul 2026 08:00:00 +0000

A seamount is a submerged underwater mountain formed by volcanic activity that acts as a vital biodiversity hotspot and influences ocean circulation patterns.

The science word of the day is "seamount" (pronounced SEE'-mount), a fundamental geological term used by oceanographers and marine scientists to describe a large underwater mountain. Unlike oceanic islands, which rise above the water surface to form land, seamounts remain completely submerged beneath the waves. These massive submarine structures typically originate from the ocean floor and rise sharply, often reaching thousands of meters in elevation, yet their peaks remain hidden hundreds or even thousands of meters beneath the surface of the sea, making them invisible from above.

Seamounts are primarily created through volcanic activity occurring deep within the Earth's crust. As tectonic plates move over geological hotspots or separate at divergent boundaries, magma erupts onto the seabed, gradually depositing layers of basaltic lava over millions of years. While the vast majority of seamounts are extinct volcanoes that have cooled over time, some remain volcanically active. These features are distributed throughout every ocean basin, with notably dense clusters situated across the vast expanse of the Pacific Ocean near tectonic boundaries.

Beyond their geological formation, seamounts play an essential role in supporting marine life and influencing ocean circulation. Their steep topographies deflect deep-sea currents upward, generating nutrient-rich upwellings that fertilize surrounding waters. This influx of nutrients attracts diverse biological communities, ranging from fragile deep-sea corals to pelagic predators such as whales, sharks, and sea turtles. Consequently, seamounts function as vital biodiversity hotspots in the vast open ocean, offering critical refuge, feeding grounds, and breeding sites for countless marine species while helping researchers better understand plate tectonics.
