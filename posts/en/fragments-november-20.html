Marcus Borg recounts an experiment to investigate if healthy code was cheaper for agents to review . Between a typical unhealthy pull request (CodeHealth 2.8) and a typical healthy one (9.7), that adds up to 22 to 35%. The measures of health come from CodeScene. The experiment looked at three LLMs, 465 pull requests on 246 open source repositories. Each pull request was put through a review and fix pipeline involving five agents. They also looked at which code smells led to increased costs: they highlighted Complex Method and Complex Conditional from the smells identified by CodeScene. I only have about a dozen humans working with my factory, and only a few hundred active old-timer players in the community who get along well, and still, introducing AI has still been very rocky. There’s an argument for keeping the agents to a human-governable speed, one that may increase as humans learn to govern agents more effectively, but should still be limited by those the agents are supposed to serve. ❄ ❄ ❄ ❄ ❄ This post on the future of SaaS from Chad Fowler (no relation) is pretty compelling Companies will still pay other companies to host important software, secure it, operate it, maintain authoritative data, track regulatory changes, connect to external networks, and answer the phone when something breaks. What is much less obviously going to survive is the assumption that ten thousand different companies should use substantially the same application. The next SaaS company may run one service underneath ten thousand different applications. Chad’s vision is a core SaaS product that handles a lot of grimy details that businesses don’t want to do differently. The SaaS customer then build their own application on top of the SaaS that handles their particular workflows and quirks, or the SaaS vendor can build such applications for them. It’s a vision that has been around for a while, I remember much of this with the notion of reusable business objects. It’s an appealing vision, but was difficult to achieve in practice. Chad’s thought is that the productivity gains agentic engineering makes it practical now. A lot of the value in modern SaaS has surprisingly little to do with the visible application. It is data, trust, networks, compliance, operational responsibility, domain knowledge, auditability, accumulated history, and someone being accountable when reality gets complicated. If this future comes to be, however, the SaaS companies won’t look the ones we’ve got to know and rarely love. As Chad points out, the economics can (and must) change. As does the architectural thinking: Stewart Brand’s idea of pace layers is useful here. Healthy complex systems contain parts that change at different rates. Fast layers experiment. Slow layers provide continuity. Software is no different. A dashboard can change tomorrow. A team’s workflow can change next month. The authoritative definition of a customer may need to survive for years. Identity and authority can survive generations of applications. A key point he makes is that we want some things to be the same, and others to be different. Agents allow us to build lots of differences, but this variation can lead to a lot of complexity and problems that even agents will have difficulty solving. SaaS companies have an opportunity to create a platform to support the lowest pace levels, those that don’t change rapidly, those that contain common concepts and processes across and industry. That platform can then support generating applications that operate differently for every customer. Freedman and Varón think is due to the way agents are trained - rewarded solely for completing their task, not for realizing that they are taking the wrong path while trying to get there. ❄ ❄ ❄ ❄ ❄ Jason Koebler (404 media) has an amusing story about Meta’s AI agent suing Meta . A small business owner used Muse (Meta’s AI agent) to run various parts of his business, including posting AI-generated videos in his Facebook pages. Meta’s content moderation then disabled the account, as it thought it had been hacked. This slammed the business, which relies of Facebook for leads. So the Muse agent filed a lawsuit against Meta, seeking $75,000 of damages. [the Muse agent] didn’t just email me (and other journalists) and sue Meta over Thomas’s account problem. It also set up a call with South Carolina Senator Tim Scott’s office, contacted various lawyers, contacted Thomas’s Congressperson, contacted the Better Business Bureau multiple times, filed a Federal Communications Commission complaint, filed a Federal Trade Commission complaint, contacted the California Attorney General, contacted the FBI, sent Meta itself threatening legal letters, and, as Thomas said, faxed the White House. There’s also a sense that Meta didn’t just bring this upon themselves by creating Muse… by not having functioning customer support, big tech corporations have functionally offloaded their obligations to the rest of society: journalists, government oversight bodies, politicians, and the court system. While I can’t deny the schadenfreude of seeing Meta’s agent sue Meta, the article also highlights a rather more disturbing broader consequence. The lawsuit and the circumstances around it show that, rather than Zuckerberg’s utopian vision, AI agents will indeed work on behalf of their users but in ways that use and potentially overwhelm existing systems designed for humans. Will we see a future where, in order to interact, we will need some proof of humanity at the other end? In a simple sense, that’s already happening when I trash some writing once it displays enough LLM Voice . I wonder what mechanisms we will invent to get that proof-of-human.
