Scientists Vote to Abolish the Leap Second to Protect Global Networks

www.newscientist.com · Fri, 16 Oct 2026 13:59:18 +0000

International scientists meeting in France have voted to abolish the leap second, ending a decades-long practice of adding irregular seconds to align atomic clocks with Earth's rotation.

International metrologists and scientists meeting in France have officially voted to scrap the "leap second," bringing an end to a half-century of adjustments designed to align atomic clocks with Earth's natural rotation. Since the system was established in 1972, global timekeepers have periodically added a total of 27 leap seconds to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). While the practice was meant to keep human clocks synchronized with the planet's fluctuating astronomical movements, representatives at the conference concluded that it has become obsolete and counterproductive in our interconnected digital world.

The primary reason for abandoning the leap second is the operational chaos it causes for modern technology. Unlike regular time changes, leap seconds cannot be anticipated years in advance because variations in Earth’s rotational speed are inherently irregular. When these extra seconds are applied, high-precision computer networks, telecommunications infrastructure, financial trading platforms, and satellite navigation systems frequently suffer synchronization glitches. In recent years, several major internet companies have reported significant system failures and outages directly attributed to the unexpected insertion of these extra seconds.

Under the historic agreement, scientists determined that the insertion of leap seconds will be halted in the coming decade, allowing atomic time and astronomical time to drift apart slightly. Metrologists emphasized that the natural deviation will remain virtually imperceptible to the general public for many decades and will not affect daily human activities. Instead, abandoning the practice will provide critical stability and consistency to global digital networks, ensuring that modern infrastructure can operate continuously without the looming threat of sudden time discrepancies.
