The official movie poster for Artificial , Luca Guadagnino’s upcoming film about OpenAI, looks like AI slop, only it was drawn by human hands. Anyone who has come across AI-generated images knows they often carry telltale mistakes. So when film studio Neon released the poster this week, users on social media were quick to catch oddities in the illustration, leading some to suspect it was made using AI. At first glance, the poster looks normal. On a closer look, Andrew Garfield, who plays Sam Altman in the movie, doesn’t appear quite like himself, lingering somewhere in the uncanny valley. The background features San Francisco’s landscape, except the Golden Gate Bridge is underwater. The tree behind Garfield looks distorted, with a Pinocchio-like face surfacing from the trunk. The branches are no less strange, morphing into snakes. The closer you look, the weirder it gets. Just a few hours after the poster was rolled out on social media, Neon followed up with a video showing a human drawing the scene by hand, crediting illustrator Mike Thompson and creative studio Gravillis. “Art made by humans,” Neon captioned the behind-the-scenes video on X. Art made by humans (Gravillis Inc. and Mike Thompson). https://t.co/0xGxxMKchF pic.twitter.com/XWwBfYU7ym — NEON (@neonrated) November 19, 2026 Users on social media quickly changed their tune, praising the studio and the movie’s creative team for their approach to the poster design. “The poster for [Artificial] being made by an actual human artist while made to look like it was AI generated wow that’s luca GODagnino,” one user said on X . Another user added, “This poster is a masterpiece. So many fun, completely bizarre, and purposeful ‘AI Slop’ touches. Zoom in and see if you can spot them all.” Many also took notice that Neon didn’t just come out and say the poster was made by humans but rather made the effort to film a behind-the-scenes video and upload it as proof. “Thank you for filming their processes and including the views of the artists in a world where what we see can be compromised or untrue,” one user said on X . People-powered Neon is not the first company to show how its work was made as a way to stand apart from AI slop. In recent months, brands across retail and tech have done the same. In August, Apple released a behind-the-scenes video of its claymation ad for the new Mac Mini. Prada’s animated holiday campaign includes sketches from the artist. But behind-the-scenes footage works only when the ads themselves are craft-forward, leaning on labor-intensive handwork as a form of effort signaling. While proof of humanness taps into the film’s theme—bringing a sense of irony by making human art look artificial—not every observer is embracing the marketing trend. One X user proclaimed , “Anybody posting any type of art now has to prove to morons that it was genuinely created by them and not AI. What a shit hole time period we live in.”
