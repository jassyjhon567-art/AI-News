Rising Longevity Reshapes How Wealthy Business Owners Approach Succession and Financial Planning

www.fastcompany.com · 2026-07-29T13:20:51

A Bank of America survey reveals that rising longevity is prompting wealthy business owners to treat succession as a gradual, decades-long process rather than a single exit event.

As average lifespans increase, wealthy entrepreneurs are finding it increasingly difficult to determine when and how to exit their companies. According to a recent Bank of America survey of high-net-worth Americans, 92 percent view longevity as a crucial factor in their overall financial strategy. Furthermore, while 78 percent of business owners recognize succession planning as critical, only 20 percent have a fully documented plan in place. This discrepancy comes at a time when multi-generational business transitions are rising sharply, creating complex exit strategies that span decades of overlapping ownership, family interests, and management responsibilities.

Financial and longevity experts emphasize that business succession should no longer be viewed as a single exit event, but rather as a gradual, long-term evolution. Many founders are adapting by building independent management teams, engaging in partial sales, or establishing employee stock ownership plans to extract liquidity while maintaining equity. Experts warn against relying solely on business wealth for retirement, noting that unexpected economic downturns or health challenges can suddenly erode a company's value. Diversifying assets through partial liquidity helps safeguard personal finances without forcing founders to fully surrender their businesses.

Beyond financial considerations, identity and family dynamics play a major role in delaying formal exit plans. Many entrepreneurs derive personal purpose from their roles, leading them to retain voting rights or strategic control even as successors take over daily operations. Additionally, many owners hesitate to formalize succession because they lack an obvious heir or incorrectly assume the next generation wants to take over. Experts stress that longevity planning must also address potential cognitive decline, yet fewer than half of survey respondents possess essential estate planning documents like wills, living wills, and durable powers of attorney.
