Ending COVID-Era Emergency Food Assistance Linked to Rise in Child Maltreatment, Study Finds

phys.org · Thu, 29 Oct 2026 00:10:01 EDT

A study reveals that ending pandemic-era SNAP emergency allotments led to increased child maltreatment rates, demonstrating how food assistance directly influences broader family and child well-being.

A recent study published in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine has identified a troubling correlation between the termination of pandemic-era food aid and increased rates of child maltreatment across the United States. Researchers found that counties that discontinued Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) Emergency Allotments experienced a measurable rise in reported child abuse and neglect cases, emphasizing the fragile balance within vulnerable households.

During the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, the federal government authorized emergency SNAP allotments to alleviate severe economic hardship and ensure adequate nutrition for low-income families. These temporary benefits served as a vital social safety net. According to the study, the abrupt loss of this financial cushion intensified household stress, pushing already struggling caregivers into deeper poverty, which frequently exacerbates conditions associated with domestic strain and child maltreatment.

The findings indicate that social safety net policies carry implications extending well beyond immediate food security. Researchers argue that consistent nutritional assistance acts as an indirect protective factor against family instability and severe domestic crises. As discussions surrounding welfare reform continue, the study highlights the critical need for policymakers to consider how alterations to public assistance programs affect overall child welfare and family health.
