Why Europe’s Political Centre Is Resilient Enough to Hold

www.ft.com · Mon, 28 Sep 2026 11:01:31 GMT

Although modern populism sparks fears of a 1930s-style collapse, Europe's robust democratic institutions, economic safety nets, and pragmatic electorates ensure that its political centre remains resilient.

Contemporary political commentators frequently compare the recent rise of populism across Europe to the democratic collapse of the 1930s. However, using the interwar era as a blueprint for understanding modern European politics is fundamentally flawed. Today’s European nations possess deep-rooted democratic traditions, strong civil societies, and extensive welfare states that buffer citizens against severe economic deprivation, unlike the fragile and impoverished systems of the early twentieth century.

Furthermore, the institutional architecture of the continent provides unprecedented stability. The European Union serves as a supranational anchor, binding member states to shared standards of the rule of law, human rights, and market integration that were entirely non-existent a century ago. While radical and populist parties have undeniably expanded their influence, they operate within established constitutional frameworks. When entering governing coalitions or seeking broader electoral relevance, many of these factions ultimately moderate their positions rather than dismantling democratic norms.

Finally, the European electorate itself is far more educated, interconnected, and pragmatic than historical analogies suggest. Voters may express frustration with traditional establishments over issues such as immigration and the cost of living, but this discontent rarely translates into an appetite for violent totalitarianism. Mainstream centrist parties have repeatedly demonstrated the capacity to adapt their policies to public concerns. As long as these institutions remain responsive, Europe's political centre will continue to absorb pressures and endure.
